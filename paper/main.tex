\begin{abstract}
The most accurate robust estimators for point cloud registration build a compatibility graph over all putative correspondences and pay this cost on every pair, although most pairs can be registered from a small, well-chosen subset.
Making them cheaper is easy; making them cheaper \emph{safely} is not: every fixed shortcut, such as fewer correspondences, sampling or fewer seeds, is tuned to one benchmark's mix of easy and hard pairs and silently loses pairs when the mix changes.
We present certified adaptive budgets (\method), a drop-in wrapper that runs an unchanged estimator on growing budgets of mutually consistent correspondences and stops as soon as a learned controller is confident; a cheap gate sends pairs that would need the full budget straight to it.
The stopping rule is certified per difficulty group with Learn-then-Test, so that with high probability at most 1\,\% of the pairs registered by the full estimator are lost, for any mix of difficulties.
We evaluate it under pre-registered protocols with blind test sets, with hand-crafted and learned descriptors; the final method is tested once on \val{v3.n.blind} indoor pairs that no earlier experiment had processed.
Around \SCP and TurboReg, \method is \val{v3.sc.th.compat.live.speed}\X and \val{v3.tr.th.compat.live.speed}\X faster on these pairs with statistically non-inferior recall, and its realized risk stays within the certified level on all \val{v3.risk.n} test conditions, whereas marginal certificates and uniform sampling exceed it.
Where nearly every pair needs the full budget, \method declines to cut instead of losing pairs, at a cost of at most \val{v3.live.min.pct}\,\% in time.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Correspondence-based registration aligns two scans from putative point correspondences proposed by local descriptors.
The robust estimator that turns them into a rigid motion is where most of the accuracy comes from, and increasingly where most of the time goes.
The most accurate estimators today, such as \SCP~\cite{chen2022sc2pcr}, MAC~\cite{zhang2023mac} and TurboReg~\cite{yan2025turboreg}, build a compatibility graph over all $N$ correspondences, at a cost that grows quadratically or cubically with $N$.
They spend this cost uniformly: every configuration fixes $N$ for every pair, and evaluations sweep it from 250 to 5{,}000~\cite{huang2021predator,qin2022geotransformer}.

Yet pairs differ enormously in difficulty.
Run on only 250 correspondences chosen by our ordering (\cref{sec:ordering}), \SCP already registers \val{sc.bh.compat.fix0.rr}\,\% of the pairs of our held-out indoor scenes in \val{sc.bh.compat.fix0.ms}\,ms, against \val{sc.bh.compat.group.full}\,\% in \val{sc.bh.compat.fix4.ms}\,ms with all 4{,}000.
Other pairs need every correspondence, and some cannot be registered at all.

\paragraphb{Cheaper is easy; safe is not.}
The obvious remedy is a cheaper fixed configuration, and \cref{fig:teaser}(a) shows that one is easily found: uniform sampling of 2{,}000 correspondences runs \SCP in \val{eq.sc.rand2000.ms} instead of \val{eq.sc.full.ms}\,ms on the held-out scenes.
But a fixed shortcut is tuned to one benchmark's mix of easy and hard pairs, and nothing tells its user what it loses: \val{eq.sc.bh.rand2000.risk}\,\% of the pairs the full estimator registers on the held-out scenes and \val{eq.sc.lo.rand2000.risk}\,\% on low-overlap 3DLoMatch (\cref{fig:teaser}(c)).
A speed-up that drops an unknown share of the pairs the trusted estimator would have registered is not one that practitioners can adopt.
What they need is a \emph{contract}: a bound on what is lost relative to the estimator they trust, which survives a change of data.

\paragraphb{Certified adaptive budgets.}
We provide such a contract with \emph{certified adaptive budgets} (\method), a drop-in wrapper around an unchanged estimator (\cref{alg:cab}).
\method runs the estimator on a ladder of growing budgets and lets a learned controller stop after any stage.
Three ingredients make this both fast and safe.
First, the budgets are filled by a \emph{compatibility-guided ordering} that puts mutually consistent correspondences first, scoring every correspondence against a small set of inlier-rich anchors at a cost far below that of the estimator's own graph (\cref{fig:qualitative}).
Second, a cheap \emph{hardness gate} recognises, before the estimator runs, pairs on which the ladder would only add cost, and runs the full budget on them directly; it can only remove losses.
Third, the controller's operating point is certified with Learn-then-Test~\cite{angelopoulos2021ltt}: with probability $1-\delta$, at most a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the pairs that the full estimator registers are lost, and this holds \emph{separately for every difficulty group}, hence for any mix of easy and hard pairs.
The second point matters in practice: marginal certificates, valid only for the mix of difficulties they were calibrated on, exceed their level when the test mix differs (up to \val{marg.risk.max}\,\% on our test sets), while difficulty-conditional ones hold on every test set.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=12pt,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A drop-in wrapper that adapts the correspondence budget of a graph-based estimator to each pair, with a certified bound on the pairs lost against the full estimator that is valid for any mixture of difficulties, and a pre-estimation gate that keeps the bound (\cref{sec:method}).
\item A two-pass anchor ordering that fills small budgets with consistent correspondences at a fraction of the cost of the full graph, and an analysis of which pairs it can rescue (\cref{sec:ordering,sec:why}).
\item Evidence under two pre-registered protocols, with blind test sets (held-out 3DMatch training scenes, KITTI raw drives outside the odometry benchmark, and for the final method pairs never processed before), hand-crafted (FPFH) and learned (FCGF) descriptors, live timing and paired tests, that the certified wrapper stays within its risk level on every test set, while marginal certificates and fixed shortcuts (fewer seeds or cliques, uniform or FastMAC-style sampling, fixed budgets) do not (\cref{sec:main,sec:shift,sec:equaltime}).
\end{itemize}
On the fresh blind pairs \method is \val{v3.sc.th.compat.live.speed}\X faster for \SCP and \val{v3.tr.th.compat.live.speed}\X for TurboReg, and on the official 3DMatch benchmark \val{v3.sc.m.compat.live.speed}\X and \val{v3.tr.m.compat.live.speed}\X.
Where the data leave nothing to save, at low overlap and on outdoor raw drives, it declines to cut rather than losing pairs, and we show that the remaining time is spent on pairs that no budget can register or that need every correspondence; where its controller cannot tell success from failure, it runs the full budget.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraphb{Graph-based robust estimators.}
Spatial compatibility between correspondence pairs~\cite{bai2021pointdsc} is the backbone of today's most accurate robust estimators.
\SCP~\cite{chen2022sc2pcr} scores pairs by second-order compatibility and grows consensus sets from spectral seeds, MAC~\cite{zhang2023mac} enumerates maximal cliques, and TurboReg~\cite{yan2025turboreg} replaces clique enumeration by pivot-guided search of 3-cliques; TEASER~\cite{yang2020teaser} and KISS-Matcher~\cite{lim2025kissmatcher} prune outliers with certifiable or graph-theoretic tests.
All of them build a graph over the $N$ putative correspondences, so their cost is set by $N$, which each configuration fixes for every pair.
FastMAC~\cite{zhang2024fastmac} reduces the number of correspondences passed to MAC by stochastic spectral sampling, but computes its sampling signal on the full second-order graph and uses one sampling ratio for all pairs; TurboReg notes that downsampling correspondences speeds up clique search at a cost in accuracy~\cite{yan2025turboreg}.
These works are complementary to ours: \method leaves the estimator unchanged, wraps any of them, and decides per pair how many correspondences it needs, with a bound on what this costs in accuracy; its ordering uses the same pairwise test, but only against a small anchor set.

\paragraphb{Sampling and stopping in RANSAC.}
RANSAC~\cite{fischler1981ransac} adapts its number of hypotheses to an estimate of the inlier ratio.
PROSAC~\cite{chum2005prosac} draws from quality-ordered correspondences, sequential tests~\cite{matas2005sprt} and preemption~\cite{nister2003preemptive} stop evaluating poor hypotheses early, MAGSAC++~\cite{barath2020magsacpp} and SupeRANSAC~\cite{barath2025superansac} improve scoring and sampling, BANSAC~\cite{piedade2023bansac} adapts sampling online, and Sch\"onberger \etal~\cite{schonberger2025fixing} show that the classical stopping criterion is miscalibrated.
These rules stop when a model seems good enough, but none bounds the accuracy lost relative to a reference estimator; we compare a certified adaptive pose search with RANSAC in \suppref{supp:caps}.

\paragraphb{Adaptive computation with guarantees.}
Early exits and adaptive computation time~\cite{graves2016act,teerapittayanon2016branchynet} let networks spend less on easy inputs, and CALM~\cite{schuster2022calm} and Fast-yet-Safe~\cite{jazbec2024fastyetsafe} certify early exits with Learn-then-Test~\cite{angelopoulos2021ltt}, a distribution-free risk-control procedure.
We bring this guarantee to robust geometric estimators, where the reference is the full estimator rather than a network's last layer, and show that its marginal form breaks under the shifts in difficulty that separate registration benchmarks.
In registration, DIPR~\cite{ai2023dipr} and BUFFER-X-Lite~\cite{lim2026bufferxlite} stop iterations or feature scales early, with hand-set thresholds and without a bound on the pairs lost.
Monitoring anytime algorithms by dynamic programming~\cite{hansen2001monitoring} and the value of computation~\cite{russell1991right} motivate our Markov controller, and our difficulty-conditional certificates follow the group-conditional (Mondrian) guarantees of conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005alrw}.

\section{Certified adaptive budgets}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/qualitative.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What the cheap budget contains.} The 250 correspondences given to the estimator on a 3DMatch test pair, drawn at their reference endpoints on the reference fragment, seen from slightly above its sensor. Left: the 250 most distinctive (Lowe ratio). Right: the 250 most consistent with inlier-rich anchors (ours). Blue: true correspondences; orange: outliers.}
\label{fig:qualitative}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Problem setup}
\label{sec:setup}
A pair of scans comes with $N$ putative correspondences $\mathcal C=\{(\mathbf p_i,\mathbf q_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ from descriptor matching, sorted by distinctiveness (Lowe ratio).
A robust estimator $E$ maps any subset of $\mathcal C$ to a rigid transformation; the standard configuration runs it once on all $N$ correspondences, the \emph{full budget}, which is our \emph{reference}.
\method runs $E$ along a ladder of budgets $N_1<\dots<N_K=N$: stage $k$ returns $\hat T_k=E(\mathcal C_{\pi,N_k})$, the estimate from the first $N_k$ correspondences of an ordering $\pi$, at cumulative cost $C_k$.
Let $s_k\in\{0,1\}$ indicate that $\hat T_k$ is correct.
A stopping policy observes signals $z_k$ after each stage and stops at $\tau$, returning $\hat T_\tau$.
We want the cheapest policy whose \emph{risk}, the fraction of pairs the reference registers but the policy does not, is at most $\varepsilon$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
\min_{\tau}\ \mathbb E[C_\tau]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad R(\tau)=\Pr\big(s_K=1,\ s_\tau=0\big)\le\varepsilon .
\end{equation}
$R$ upper-bounds the recall lost against the standard estimator.
Measuring the risk against the reference rather than against ground truth isolates the price of adaptivity: \method does not claim to fix the estimator's failures, only to bound how many of its successes it gives up, which is what a practitioner who has chosen an estimator cares about.
Ground truth is needed only on calibration pairs, never at test time.
Two things decide how cheap a certified policy can be: how often the early stages already succeed, which depends on the ordering $\pi$ (\cref{sec:ordering}), and how well the controller recognises those successes (\cref{sec:controller,sec:certification}).

\subsection{Compatibility-guided ordering}
\label{sec:ordering}
Distinctiveness is a poor filter for a small budget: on the 3DMatch validation scenes, only \val{abl.top250.ratio} of the 250 most distinctive correspondences are inliers on average.
Inliers, however, are mutually consistent.
Rigidity implies $\big|\|\mathbf p_i-\mathbf p_j\|-\|\mathbf q_i-\mathbf q_j\|\big|<d$ for two inliers $i,j$, where $d$ is the estimator's compatibility threshold; let $C_{ij}$ indicate this test.
Scoring all pairs $(i,j)$ costs $O(N^2)$, as much as the estimator itself.
We instead score every correspondence against a small set $A$ of anchors with second-order support,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
s_i=\sum_{a\in A} C_{ia}\,\big(C\,C_{AA}\big)_{ia},
\end{equation}
the number of anchor triangles that $i$ closes, and fill each budget with the highest-scoring correspondences (ties keep the ratio order).
A budget that covers all correspondences keeps the standard input, so the full stage is exactly the reference.

\paragraphb{Anchors decide.}
The obvious anchors, the $m=100$ most distinctive correspondences, fail exactly where help is needed: in hard pairs they are nearly all outliers (\val{abl.ratio.top100.true} inliers on average and none in \val{abl.ratio.top100.zero}\,\% of validation pairs), and outliers that happen to agree with each other are promoted.
We therefore select anchors in a first pass: among the $P=500$ most distinctive correspondences, the $m=100$ with the highest first-order degree $\sum_{j\le P}C_{ij}$ become anchors, which raises the share of inliers among anchors to \val{abl.anchors.true}\,\%.
The two passes cost $O(P^2+Nm^2)$ instead of the $O(N^2)$ to $O(N^3)$ of the estimators' graphs, \val{abl.torder.micro}\,ms per pair on one CPU core (\cref{fig:qualitative}).

\subsection{Stopping controller}
\label{sec:controller}
After each stage the controller observes estimator-agnostic signals: the estimator's inlier count; the fraction of \emph{all} $N$ correspondences, and of the 250 most distinctive, explained by $\hat T_k$; the change of $\hat T_k$ since the previous stage; the gain in explained correspondences; the stage index, $\log N$ and the median Lowe ratio of the budget.
Two classifiers fitted on training pairs predict $p_\text{now}=\Pr(s_k{=}1\mid z_k)$ and the value of continuing, $p_\text{fin}=\Pr(s_K{=}1\mid z_k)$; their kind (logistic regression with two regularisation strengths, quadratic logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees) is chosen with the ladder on the fitting pairs (\cref{sec:certification}).
We consider three policy families: a \emph{threshold} rule that stops when $p_\text{now}\ge\lambda$; \emph{accept-or-give-up}, which also stops when $p_\text{fin}<\gamma$; and a \emph{Markov} controller, a finite-horizon decision process over (stage, quintile of $p_\text{now}$, tercile of $p_\text{fin}$) whose transitions and costs are estimated on training trajectories and which is solved by backward induction~\cite{puterman1994mdp} for the reward $s-\lambda C$.
The ladder itself is a design choice: candidates are increasing subsets of $\{250,500,1000,2000,4000\}$, including ladders that end below the full budget.

\paragraphb{Hardness gate.}
Some pairs pay for every stage of the ladder and end at the full budget anyway.
Before the estimator runs, the gate computes cheap signals of the correspondences, the first pass of the ordering (anchor degrees), the anchors' second-order support of all correspondences and Lowe-ratio statistics, and a classifier predicts whether \method would cost more than the full budget on the pair; if so, the full budget runs directly.
The gated result is the reference itself, so the gate can only remove losses and needs no certificate of its own: every guarantee below holds for the gated policy unchanged.
Its classifier and threshold minimise the out-of-fold total cost on the fitting and calibration pairs.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\method at test time}
\label{alg:cab}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require correspondences $\mathcal C$ (ratio order), estimator $E$, ladder $N_1<\dots<N_K$, models $p_\text{now}$, group edges, certified thresholds $\lambda_g$
\State $\pi,\,h\gets$ two-pass compatibility order of $\mathcal C$ (\cref{eq:score}) and gate signals
\If{gate$(h)$} \Return $E(\mathcal C)$ \Comment{the full budget, directly} \EndIf
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $\hat T_k\gets E(\text{first }N_k\text{ of }\pi(\mathcal C))$ \Comment{all of $\mathcal C$, in ratio order, if $N_k\ge|\mathcal C|$}
  \State $z_k\gets$ signals of $\hat T_k$ on $\mathcal C$ and change from $\hat T_{k-1}$
  \If{$k=1$} $g\gets$ difficulty group of $z_1$ \EndIf
  \If{$p_\text{now}(z_k)\ge\lambda_g$ \textbf{or} $k=K$} \Return $\hat T_k$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Certification}
\label{sec:certification}
Every policy family is indexed by a parameter ordered from conservative to aggressive.
On $n$ held-out calibration pairs, Learn-then-Test~\cite{angelopoulos2021ltt} tests $H_\lambda: R(\lambda)>\varepsilon$ in this fixed order with exact binomial $p$-values and keeps the most aggressive $\lambda$ before the first non-rejection; then $\Pr\big(R(\hat\lambda)\le\varepsilon\big)\ge 1-\delta$ for test pairs exchangeable with the calibration pairs.
We use $\varepsilon=1$\,\% and $\delta=0.1$.
A certificate needs data: even with no loss among $n$ calibration pairs, the test rejects only if $(1-\varepsilon)^n\le\delta$, \ie for $n\ge230$ here.

\paragraphb{Difficulty-conditional certificates.}
Benchmark splits differ mostly in their mix of easy and hard pairs, which breaks exchangeability between calibration and test pairs.
We therefore partition pairs into $G$ groups by an observable after the first stage, the number of explained correspondences, at its terciles on the fitting pairs, and certify one threshold $\lambda_g$ per group, each on that group's calibration pairs at level $\delta/G$ (which needs at least 339 pairs per group for $G=3$).
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:groups}
If, within every group $g$, test pairs are exchangeable with that group's calibration pairs, then with probability at least $1-\delta$ the policy with thresholds $(\hat\lambda_g)_g$ has $R\le\varepsilon$ on every test distribution, whatever its group proportions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By a union bound over the $G$ group-wise guarantees, $R_g(\hat\lambda_g)\le\varepsilon$ holds for all $g$ simultaneously with probability $\ge1-\delta$; then $R=\sum_g w_g R_g\le\varepsilon$ for any proportions $w_g$ (full proof in \suppref{supp:proofs}).
\end{proof}
A marginal certificate only covers test sets with the calibration mix of difficulties; the conditional one covers any mix, at the price of a smaller $\delta$ per group and hence more calibration pairs.

\paragraphb{Choose first, then certify.}
The guarantee covers one sequence of tests fixed in advance, so the ladder and the policy family are chosen before any calibration pair is used.
On the fitting pairs, scored out of fold by controllers fitted on the other folds, every candidate (ladder, policy family and classifier kind) gets the most aggressive parameters whose losses stay within what the certificate can accept for the calibration set's size (none in any group, with our pool sizes), and we keep the cheapest candidate; only this one is certified, and the result is evaluated once on test.
Certifying several candidates on the same calibration pairs and keeping the cheapest would void the guarantee, because the cheapest is the most likely to have been certified by chance.
A ladder that stops below the full budget is risky even when it never stops early, so its always-continue rule is the first element of its sequence, and if that rule is rejected \method falls back to the full budget.
The full budget alone is always a candidate and needs no certificate: when nothing cheaper can be certified, for lack of calibration data or of a reliable controller, \method is the standard estimator.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:expsetup}

\paragraphb{Protocol.}
All data splits, rules and configurations were pre-registered before the data were downloaded or processed, in two stages (code release, \suppref{supp:impl}).
Protocol~v2 evaluated \method without the gate, with the logistic controller, once on blind held-out scenes and KITTI raw drives.
The gate and the classifier choice were then found on development data only, so protocol~v3 tested the final method once on a fresh blind test: \val{v3.n.blind} pairs of \val{v3.n.scenes} further 3DMatch training scenes that no experiment had processed before, under any role (\val{v3.n.dropped} of the \val{v3.n.sampled} sampled pairs had appeared in earlier logs as fitting or training pairs and were removed before any was processed).
The final method was fitted on fitting pairs outside these scenes.
The protocol-v2 blind sets and the official benchmarks, which earlier versions of this work had evaluated, are reported for the final method as earlier test sets, not as blind tests.

\paragraphb{Indoor data.}
The 75 3DMatch~\cite{zeng2017_3dmatch} training scenes are permuted with a fixed seed: 9 scenes form the blind test set, 13 provide \val{v2_indoor_fpfh.n.cal} calibration pairs, and the rest \val{v2_indoor_fpfh.n.fit} fitting pairs; pairs overlap by more than 10\,\%.
The blind pairs are reported in two parts, above 30\,\% overlap (\val{bh.n.test} pairs, 3DMatch-like) and between 10 and 30\,\% (\val{bl.n.test}, 3DLoMatch-like); the fresh blind test of protocol~v3 likewise (\val{th.n.test} and \val{tl.n.test} pairs).
Development benchmarks: the official 3DMatch (\val{m.n.test}) and 3DLoMatch~\cite{huang2021predator} (\val{lo.n.test}) test pairs, with the same fitting and calibration pairs.

\paragraphb{Outdoor data.}
The \val{kr.n.test} blind pairs come from the 49 KITTI~\cite{geiger2012kitti} raw drives that belong to none of the odometry sequences 0--10, paired by FCGF's rule~\cite{choy2019fcgf} with GPS/IMU poses refined by ICP; 22 pairs on which the refinement failed were dropped by a pre-registered rule.
\val{v2_kitti_fpfh.n.fit} odometry training pairs fit the controller and \val{v2_kitti_fpfh.n.cal} other odometry training and validation pairs certify it; the \val{ki.n.test} odometry test pairs are the development benchmark.

\paragraphb{Correspondences and estimators.}
FPFH~\cite{rusu2009fpfh} descriptors (5\,cm voxels indoors, 30\,cm outdoors); for every source point the nearest target descriptor, sorted by Lowe ratio and capped at $N=4{,}000$.
FCGF~\cite{choy2019fcgf} features are computed for every split with the official weights by our re-implementation of the network, which reproduces the released test features up to rounding (\suppref{supp:impl}).
FCGF was trained on all 3DMatch training scenes and on odometry sequences 0--5, so it has no blind indoor test; it is fitted on the indoor fitting pairs, certified on the \val{v2_indoor_fcgf.n.cal} pairs of the 3DMatch validation scenes and tested on the development benchmarks, and outdoors it is certified on \val{v2_kitti_fcgf.n.cal} pairs of 42 raw drives and tested blind on the \val{fkr.n.test} pairs of the other 7.
\SCP~\cite{chen2022sc2pcr} and TurboReg~\cite{yan2025turboreg} are re-implemented on CPU.
At the full budget they come within \val{fid.fcgf.indoor.gap.min}--\val{fid.fcgf.indoor.gap.max}\pp of the published recall on 3DMatch and 3DLoMatch with FCGF, and within \val{fid.fpfh.indoor.gap.min}--\val{fid.fpfh.indoor.gap.max}\pp with FPFH (\suppref{supp:impl}).

\paragraphb{Metrics.}
A pair is registered if RE${}\le15^\circ$ and TE${}\le30$\,cm indoors, and RE${}\le5^\circ$ and TE${}\le60$\,cm outdoors; recall (RR) is over all pairs.
The \emph{loss} of a configuration is the recall lost against the full budget with its one-sided 95\,\% upper bound (scene-stratified bootstrap~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}); it is \emph{non-inferior} if the bound is below 1\pp (2\pp at low overlap), and its \emph{risk} is the fraction of pairs registered by the full budget but not by \method.

\paragraphb{Cost.}
\emph{Wall-clock speed-ups} are measured \emph{live}: one process with the default multi-threaded BLAS on a laptop CPU (AMD Ryzen~7 5700U, 8 cores), the same \val{sc.m.live.n} random test pairs per set for every configuration, and the order of the runs rotated from pair to pair.
Each run includes everything the configuration needs, such as the ordering in \method or the sampling signal in FastMAC.
Because time depends on the machine, we also count \emph{operations}: the dominant arithmetic of every stage a pair visits (compatibility tests, graph products, consensus and hypothesis scoring, $O(N^2)$ to $O(N^3)$ in the budget $N$) plus the $O(P^2+Nm^2)$ of our ordering, averaged over the full test set (\suppref{supp:impl}).
Live and offline stopping decisions agree on at least \val{live.agree.min}\,\% of the pairs in every live run.

\subsection{Main results}
\label{sec:main}
\Cref{tab:blind} reports the fresh blind test, with every choice made on the fitting pairs and every certificate computed on the calibration pairs before any blind pair was processed.
On pairs above 30\,\% overlap, \method runs \SCP \val{v3.sc.th.compat.live.speed}\X and TurboReg \val{v3.tr.th.compat.live.speed}\X faster than the standard full budget (\val{v3.sc.th.compat.group.ops}\X and \val{v3.tr.th.compat.group.ops}\X fewer operations) and loses \val{v3.sc.th.compat.group.loss} and \val{v3.tr.th.compat.group.loss}\pp of recall, with one-sided 95\,\% bounds of \val{v3.sc.th.compat.group.ub} and \val{v3.tr.th.compat.group.ub}\pp: both are statistically non-inferior, and the realized risks of \val{v3.sc.th.compat.group.risk} and \val{v3.tr.th.compat.group.risk}\,\% are inside the certified 1\,\%.
At low overlap the gate sends most pairs to the full budget directly, and \method is \val{v3.sc.tl.compat.live.speed}\X and \val{v3.tr.tl.compat.live.speed}\X faster, no longer slower than the full budget.
Against \method without the gate (v2) on the same pairs and clock, the final method is \val{v3.tr.th.compat.live.vs2}\X [\val{v3.tr.th.compat.live.vs2lo}, \val{v3.tr.th.compat.live.vs2hi}] as fast for TurboReg above 30\,\% overlap and \val{v3.sc.tl.compat.live.vs2}\X and \val{v3.tr.tl.compat.live.vs2}\X at low overlap, but \val{v3.sc.th.compat.live.vs2}\X [\val{v3.sc.th.compat.live.vs2lo}, \val{v3.sc.th.compat.live.vs2hi}] for \SCP above 30\,\% overlap: there, on the pairs it lets through, it nearly always stops where \method without the gate stops, and the gate's live overhead outweighs its saving (\cref{sec:conclusion}); offline, where the gate costs \val{gate.signals.ms}\,ms of signals that replace the ordering's own \val{gate.order.ms}\,ms and a prediction of \val{gate.pred.hgb.ms}\,ms, it is \val{v3.sc.th.compat.group.speed}\X against \val{sc.th.compat.group.speed}\X.
On every earlier test set (\cref{tab:main}) the final method stays within the risk level and is non-inferior.
It is faster than \method without the gate for TurboReg on every set where it cuts, and for \SCP on most, with the largest change on the KITTI raw drives (\val{v3.sc.kr.compat.live.speed}\X and \val{v3.tr.kr.compat.live.speed}\X, against \val{sc.kr.compat.live.speed}\X and \val{tr.kr.compat.live.speed}\X), where the gate removes the slowdown of protocol~v2; for \SCP it is slightly slower on 3DMatch and on FCGF 3DLoMatch.

\subsection{The guarantee under shift}
\label{sec:shift}
A certificate is only worth having if it holds on data that differ from the calibration data.
\Cref{fig:teaser}(c) collects the realized risk on every test set: \method stays within the 1\,\% level in all \val{v3.risk.n} cases (at most \val{v3.risk.max}\,\%).
Two alternatives do not, each for a different reason.

\emph{Marginal certificates} hold only for the calibration mix of difficulties.
With the final method they exceed their level on 3DMatch and on FCGF 3DMatch (\val{v3.sc.m.compat.marg.risk} and \val{v3.sc.fm.compat.marg.risk}\,\% for \SCP), and in protocol~v2 they did on the blind KITTI raw drives, whose pairs are much harder than the odometry calibration pairs (\val{sc.kr.compat.group.full} vs.\ \val{sc.ki.compat.group.full}\,\% full-budget recall): \val{tr.kr.ratio.marg.risk}\,\% for TurboReg with the ratio order.
The group-conditional certificates stay within the level in every case, as \cref{prop:groups} predicts.
In the protocol-v2 configuration, the marginal certificates' test risk within one difficulty group reaches \val{ev.worst.marg.fpfh.max}\,\%, against at most \val{ev.worst.group.fpfh.max}\,\% for the group-conditional ones on the FPFH test sets (\suppref{supp:limits}), and on KITTI odometry a single threshold loses \val{cal.tr.kitti_test.marginal.pool}\,\% of TurboReg's pairs, where the group-conditional thresholds lose \val{cal.tr.kitti_test.mondrian.pool}\,\%.

\emph{Fixed shortcuts} carry no certificate.
Uniform sampling of 2{,}000 correspondences loses \val{eq.sc.bh.rand2000.risk} and \val{eq.tr.bh.rand2000.risk}\,\% of the pairs registered by the full estimator on the held-out scenes, and \val{eq.sc.lo.rand2000.risk} and \val{eq.tr.lo.rand2000.risk}\,\% on 3DLoMatch (\cref{sec:equaltime}).

\emph{Learned descriptors.}
With FCGF, the controller and its certificate are fitted on FCGF features, and the guarantee holds again (risks of at most \val{v3.risk.max}\,\% over all test sets), but the savings are small (\cref{tab:main}): the pairs FCGF solves are solved by the first stage already, and its few failures are hard to tell from successes.
On the KITTI raw drives the controller cannot tell them apart at all: its fitting pairs are FCGF's own training pairs, on which nearly every first stage succeeds, so it learns no failure signal; the certificate rejects every early stop on the calibration drives, and \method runs the full budget, although 250 correspondences already register \val{sc.fkr.compat.fix0.rr}\,\% of the blind pairs.
A certificate thus protects against a weak controller, at the price of speed; it cannot replace fitting data with failures.

\subsection{Equal-time comparison with cheaper estimators}
\label{sec:equaltime}
Is adaptivity needed, or would any cheaper way of running the estimator do as well?
\Cref{fig:teaser,tab:equaltime} compare \method with five alternatives on the same pairs and the same live clock.
\emph{Fewer seeds or cliques} barely save time: the cheapest settings still take \val{eq.sc.cheap.min.ms} and \val{eq.tr.cheap.min.ms}\,ms (full budget: \val{eq.sc.full.ms} and \val{eq.tr.full.ms}\,ms), because building the compatibility graph over all correspondences dominates.
Neither does \emph{FastMAC-style sampling} as published: its signal on the full second-order graph alone takes \val{eq.sc.fm2.signal.ms}\,ms on a CPU; on the first-order graph the signal costs \val{eq.sc.fm1.signal.ms}\,ms, and at \method's time this variant registers \val{eq.sc.fm1.gapu}\pp fewer pairs (\SCP).
\emph{Fixed budgets} reach any time but trail \method by \val{eq.sc.ratio.gapu}--\val{eq.sc.compat.gapu}\pp (\SCP) and \val{eq.tr.compat.gapu}--\val{eq.tr.ratio.gapu}\pp (TurboReg) at its time, because a fixed rule must serve the hardest pairs it wants to keep, while \method spends the extra correspondences only where the first budget failed.
The strongest baseline is \emph{uniform sampling}: at \method's time it registers \val{eq.sc.rand.gapu} and \val{eq.tr.rand.gapu}\pp fewer pairs (\SCP, TurboReg).
Its cheapest competitive setting, $N=2{,}000$ random correspondences, is faster than \method (\val{eq.sc.rand2000.ms} against \val{eq.sc.cab.ms}\,ms for \SCP) but registers fewer pairs: \val{eq.sc.bh.rand2000.rr} against \val{eq.sc.bh.cab.rr}\,\% (McNemar $p=\val{eq.sc.bh.rand2000.p}$), and for TurboReg \val{eq.tr.bh.rand2000.rr} against \val{eq.tr.bh.cab.rr}\,\% ($p=\val{eq.tr.bh.rand2000.p}$).
On 3DLoMatch (\suppref{supp:equaltime}) there is nothing to win at equal time: every cheaper variant that saves at least 5\,\% of the time loses at least \val{eq.sc.lo.minloss}\pp (\SCP) and \val{eq.tr.lo.minloss}\pp (TurboReg) of recall, whereas \method, whose certificate bounds the loss at 1\pp, correctly runs almost every pair to the full budget (\val{eq.sc.lo.cab.trel}\X and \val{eq.tr.lo.cab.trel}\X the full time).

\paragraphb{When it pays off.}

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\paragraphb{Anchors.}
Two passes are what make the ordering work (\supptabref{tab:ablation}): with the most distinctive correspondences as anchors, one pass triples the inliers in the cheap budget but barely changes \SCP's success at $N=250$ and lowers it at 500 and 1{,}000, while two passes gain \val{abl.sc2.k0.gain.two} and \val{abl.sc2.k1.gain.two}\pp at $N=250$ and 500.

\paragraphb{Certificates.}
With marginal certificates \method without the gate is faster on the held-out scenes (\val{sc.bh.compat.marg.speed}\X and \val{tr.bh.compat.marg.speed}\X offline, against \val{sc.bh.compat.group.speed}\X and \val{tr.bh.compat.group.speed}\X), but these certificates exceed their level elsewhere (\cref{sec:shift}); varying the risk level and the number of groups trades speed for risk smoothly, with the realized risk always below $\varepsilon$ (\suppref{supp:limits}).

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraphb{Limitations.}
Our estimators are CPU re-implementations; on a GPU the cost gap between small and large budgets narrows, and the speed-ups must be measured again.
\method lowers the mean time per pair, not the worst case: a pair that needs the whole ladder also pays for the stages tried before, up to \val{v3.lat.p99.max}\,\% more than one full run at the 99th percentile (\val{v3.lat.p99.where}).
In our implementation the gate costs \val{v3.gate.live.overhead.min}--\val{v3.gate.live.overhead.max}\,ms per pair live (median over pairs with identical decisions), against \val{gate.pred.hgb.ms}\,ms for its prediction timed alone, and \val{gate.dev.overhead.min}--\val{gate.dev.overhead.max}\,ms on development pairs, where we could not trace it to the classifier's threads or to the timing harness; it makes \method slower than without the gate for \SCP above 30\,\% overlap, and the blind results include it.
Only the fresh test is blind for the final method, whose gate and classifier choice were found on development data after the protocol-v2 evaluation; it is indoor and FPFH only, and its scenes are fresh with respect to the data that fit, calibrate and choose the final method, but every 3DMatch training scene had appeared in earlier logs.
A certificate needs calibration data, and its speed needs fitting data with failures: on random calibration subsets, the group-conditional certificate appeared only from about a thousand pairs (\suppref{supp:limits}), and with FCGF on KITTI, whose fitting pairs are FCGF's own training pairs, \method can only run the full budget; FCGF has seen every 3DMatch training scene, so learned descriptors are tested blind only outdoors.

\paragraphb{Conclusion.}
Not every pair needs every correspondence, but a cheaper estimator is only useful if one knows what it loses.
Consistency-ordered budgets, a difficulty-conditional certificate of a candidate chosen beforehand and a pre-estimation gate make graph-based estimators markedly faster where the data allow, at statistically non-inferior recall and within the certified risk on every test set, including pairs that no experiment had processed before; marginal certificates and fixed shortcuts give no such assurance.
A certificate is only as fast as its controller, which needs fitting data with failures, and an improvement found after a blind evaluation needs a new one: the gate held for TurboReg and at low overlap, but not for \SCP above 30\,\% overlap.
The headroom left (\supptabref{tab:headroom}) lies in correct poses that the controller does not yet recognise and in pairs that no budget can register: sharper verification of each stage's pose, rather than the choice of budget, is the next step.
